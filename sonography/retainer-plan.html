Your credential is the business. Stop renting it out by the shift.

Two decades of vascular scanning is a licensed, scarce, hard-to-replace skill — and it is currently earning like hourly labour. The goal here is the same skill, sold as a monthly commitment instead of a shift, at a scale that fits around everything else you do.

The structure

Two revenue lines, and only one of them is scanning

Per-diem shifts will hit your number, but they stop the moment you stop. The plan below runs one scanning line for immediate cash and a second line that is genuinely recurring — and the first line is how you find customers for the second.

Line A — Contract scan sessions

Cash now

Small practices want vascular studies in-house but cannot justify a full-time tech. You cover a standing half-day — the same afternoon every week — at their facility, on their machine. They bill the study; you invoice a flat session rate.

Best fits: vein and varicose clinics, nephrology and dialysis access centres, podiatry practices running diabetic PAD screening, wound care, small cardiology groups.

Line B — Accreditation & QA retainer

The engine

Vascular labs carry IAC accreditation, and it obliges them to run a documented quality assurance programme — correlation reviews, technical standards, protocol manuals, reaccreditation every three years. Small practices are consistently bad at this and know it.

You run their QA as a monthly retainer. Mostly remote. A few hours a month, not a week.

Why this combination works

Every practice you scan at is a qualified prospect for the retainer — you are already inside, you have seen their protocols, and you know exactly where their documentation is thin. Line A is not just income, it is your sales pipeline for Line B. Most people in your position only ever run Line A.

There is a second, quieter reason Line B is valuable to them: accreditation requires appropriately credentialed technical staff. A dual-registered RVT attached to their lab is not just a service they buy — it is a compliance box your presence helps close.

Note what the mix does to your risk. If the scanning contract ends, you keep $1,500 of retainer revenue and roughly four hours a week back to replace it. If a retainer lapses, you lose $750 and three hours a month. No single client can take out more than half your income, which is the actual definition of the low-risk you asked for.

The retainers are also the only part that compounds. A fourth and fifth lab cost you three hours each and no new selling effort once you have a reference client — that is the path from $3,000 to $5,000 without touching the time budget.

Where to get the list

The IAC publishes a directory of accredited facilities. Filter to your commuting radius and you have a targeted prospect list for Line B — with the added advantage that facilities approaching their three-year reaccreditation are the warmest possible leads. Cross-reference against practices you or your colleagues already know from twenty years in the field, and start there.

Client acquisition

Getting the first three

You are not cold-selling a novel service. You are a known professional in a small regional community offering to solve a problem the buyer already knows they have. That means direct contact, not marketing.

Skip the website. Skip the logo. A one-page rate sheet and thirty phone calls will outperform anything you could build.

The scanning approach

Call the practice manager, not the physician

“Hi, I'm an RDMS and RVT with twenty years of vascular experience, working independently in the area. I'm reaching out because practices your size often want to bring vascular studies in-house but can't justify a full-time tech.

I cover standing half-day sessions — same afternoon each week, on your equipment, flat session rate, I carry my own malpractice. If your referring volume would support four hours a week, it's usually worth a conversation. Would it make sense to look at your numbers together?”

The retainer approach

Ask about the pain before you pitch

“Quick question — who handles your QA documentation and correlation reviews for accreditation right now?”

Wait. The answer is almost always an uncomfortable pause, a physician doing it badly at midnight, or a tech who left.

“That's what I hear most places. I run that as a monthly service for a few labs — correlation log, QA reviews, protocol manual kept current, and I keep you audit-ready so reaccreditation isn't a fire drill. Flat monthly, mostly remote. Want me to look at where you stand?”

The free diagnostic is your closer. Offer to review their current QA documentation at no charge and tell them what would fail an audit. It costs you an hour, it demonstrates twenty years of expertise better than any credential list, and practices rarely walk away from someone who has just shown them a live compliance gap.

Setup

What you need before the first invoice

Do this

~$600–900
  • Confirm New Jersey licensure requirements for independent sonography practice — several states now license sonographers separately from national registry, and rules have changed recently. Verify before you contract, not after.
  • Form a single-member LLC and get an EIN. Cheap, fast, and it keeps personal assets separate.
  • Carry your own professional liability policy. Do not rely on the practice's coverage as a contractor — theirs may not extend to you.
  • Written contractor agreement with each practice. New Jersey applies a strict test to independent contractor status, so having multiple clients, your own insurance and a real agreement all matter.
  • Charge flat time-based rates — per session or per month, not per study tied to volume. Cleaner and simpler to defend.
  • One-page rate sheet. Credentials, services, rates, coverage. That is your entire marketing budget.

Do not do this

Risk
  • Don't buy an ultrasound machine. Fifteen to forty thousand dollars converts a zero-risk business into a leveraged one overnight. Only revisit once a client has committed to volume that pays for it.
  • Don't open an elective 3D/4D keepsake studio. Capital-heavy, storefront lease, constant marketing, and non-medical fetal imaging is discouraged by regulators. It fails every constraint you set.
  • Don't run direct-to-consumer screening events without a physician interpretation arrangement in place. Your community network makes this tempting — the scope-of-practice and liability exposure makes it a bad first move.
  • Never interpret. You scan and provide technical findings; the physician reads and diagnoses. That line protects your licence, and blurring it is the fastest way to lose everything else here.
  • Don't take equity or revenue share in place of a rate. You are buying predictability, which is the entire point.
Execution

First ninety days

  1. Weeks 1–2 — Get legal and get a list

    Verify licensure requirements, form the LLC, bind the insurance policy, write the one-page rate sheet. In parallel, build a list of thirty target practices within a thirty-minute drive, cross-referenced against the IAC accredited-facility directory.

  2. Weeks 3–6 — Ten calls a week, scanning first

    Lead with the contract scanning offer, because it is the easier yes and it gets you physically inside practices. Target: one standing weekly session signed. That alone is roughly half your income goal.

  3. Weeks 6–10 — Convert from the inside

    Once you are scanning somewhere, ask the QA question. Offer the free documentation review. Your first retainer should come from a practice that already knows your work — that is a far shorter sale than a cold one.

  4. Weeks 10–13 — Second retainer, then stop selling

    Use the first retainer client as a reference and land a second. At three clients you are at target. Hold there deliberately for a quarter and let the systems settle before adding anyone.

The honest risk

Nothing in this plan is likely to fail on the numbers. The realistic failure mode is the one visible in this conversation: a new idea arriving in month two and pulling attention away before the third client is signed. Ninety days of narrow focus is genuinely the hardest requirement on this page — harder than any of the arithmetic.
