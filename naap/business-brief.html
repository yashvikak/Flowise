Working name — Naap Made-to-measure ethnic wear, India → diaspora Pre-seed brief

What it takes to put an Indian tailor in every diaspora closet

The demand is obvious and the supply exists. What sits between them is not a website — it’s customs paperwork, a measurement problem, and a promise about a wedding date. This brief is about what it actually costs to stand in that gap.

The readWhere the intuition is right, and the one place it is wrong
Reframing the premise

You are not selling cheap. You are selling fit and certainty.

The instinct — it’s so easy and so cheap in India — is the right observation about the world and the wrong basis for the business. It was a viable arbitrage until recently. It is not one now.

Through mid-2025, a garment worth under $800 could enter the United States duty-free as a low-value parcel. That exemption was withdrawn — first for China, then for all origins from late August 2025. Every parcel now owes duty, and apparel duty is not a rounding error: depending on fibre content, US rates on ready-made garments run from under 1% on silk to roughly 15–32% on man-made fibres, which is exactly what most lehengas, gowns and party wear are cut from. On top of that sits whatever additional India-specific tariff is in force at the time you ship, and through 2025 that number was punitively high and politically volatile.

Load-bearing claim

You cannot deliver Indian prices to a foreign doorstep any more. The freight and the duty are real and they are yours to absorb or to pass on. So don’t compete with India on price — compete with the diaspora tailor on fit, on certainty, and on the absence of nasty surprises.

That competitor is a much softer target. A woman in Edison or Southall or Brampton who wants a blouse that actually fits today has three options, and all three are bad: wait for a trip to India; hand fabric to a relative flying out; or take it to the local tailor, who charges $150–400 for what costs ₹1,500 in Lajpat Nagar and is booked eight weeks deep from September through February. She is already paying near-Western prices. She is just paying them for a worse outcome.

That is the price umbrella you sell under. Not ₹1,500. Something like $300–450 for a made-to-measure outfit that arrives finished, on a guaranteed date, with the duty already paid and no courier calling for money at the door.

The gapTwo compliance burdens, one entity that absorbs both
Structural insight

The real product is being the exporter and the importer, so nobody else has to be

The reason those Delhi and Jaipur boutiques aren’t already selling overseas isn’t that they lack a storefront. It’s that legally exporting from India is a specialist job that a twelve-person boutique cannot staff. It needs an IEC (Importer Exporter Code), a GST registration with a Letter of Undertaking so exports are zero-rated and input tax is recoverable, a designated AD bank to receive foreign currency under FEMA rules, and an e-BRC closing out every single shipment to prove the money came home. Miss the paperwork and the money is stuck.

Meanwhile on the receiving end, the customer is not equipped to be an importer either — which is what a direct-from-India parcel silently makes her, right up to the moment the courier bills her for duty she didn’t expect.

The wedge, precisely stated

Stand up two entities. An India private limited company that buys from boutiques as a domestic sale — so the vendor just raises a normal GST invoice and is done — and exports under one IEC, one LUT, one bank relationship. And a destination-market company that is the importer of record, clears goods in consolidated commercial entries through a licensed broker, and sells to the customer at a landed, duty-paid price.

The vendor gets export demand with zero export burden. The customer gets a domestic purchase. You are the only one touching a customs form. That is the gap, and it is a much better moat than a marketplace UI.

It also changes the revenue model. Don’t take a marketplace commission — become the merchant of record. Buy at wholesale, sell at retail, roughly a 2.2–2.6× markup. You are carrying the customs risk, the QC risk and the fit guarantee, so you need control of the price and the margin, not a 20% clip of someone else’s.

Recommendation

Start in the US because that’s where you can actually sell, but build the operating entity so the destination is swappable from day one — India export company, destination importer, broker relationship. Adding the UK later should be a new importer entity and a new broker, not a rebuild. If US tariffs stay punitive, that optionality is the difference between pivoting and dying.

FitThe hard technical problem and the one that earns the premium
Operations

Measurement is the product. Everything else is logistics.

Getting a lehenga skirt right remotely is not that hard. Getting a saree blouse right remotely is very hard — it is a fitted, darted garment with no ease, and it is the single most common reason a custom order fails. If you don’t solve this, you have a shipping company with a bad refund rate.

Four approaches, in ascending order of how much they actually work:

  • Self-measurement with a tape. Cheapest, and the least reliable. People measure over clothes, pull too tight, and misplace the bust point. Expect a high first-attempt failure rate on fitted garments.
  • Body-scan apps (3DLOOK, MySize and similar, licensed per scan). Good for circumference, weak on the precise vertical placements — shoulder-to-apex, dart depth — that fitted ethnic wear depends on.
  • Reference-garment measurement. The customer sends photos and flat measurements of a blouse or kurta that already fits her well. This is what competent remote tailors actually do, and it is dramatically more reliable than measuring a body, because it captures fit preference alongside dimension.
  • Live video fitting with the tailor in India. A twenty-minute call costs you a couple of hundred rupees of tailor time and does more for conversion and trust than any amount of website.
Design decision

Combine the last two, and turn the output into a persistent fit profile that improves with every order — measurements, reference-garment photos, and post-delivery feedback (sleeve was tight, hem an inch long) fed back as corrections. The first order is your hardest. The fifth should be one click. That accumulating profile is the retention mechanic and the thing a competitor cannot copy by cloning your site.

Then guarantee it, and reserve for it. Budget 8–12% of revenue in year one for remakes and local alteration credit, falling as the profiles mature. Line up alteration partners in your top metros — a $60 local alteration is far cheaper than a remake shipped from Delhi, and it saves the wedding date, which is the thing that actually matters.

WedgeSell to the wedding party, not the individual
Go to market

The distribution advantage you already have

A national pageant title is a real go-to-market asset and should be used deliberately rather than decoratively. It reaches precisely the demographic that buys this — Indian-American women, 25–45, socially visible, attending multiple events a season where being well-dressed is the point. Titleholders across years and states are a ready-made ambassador network with credibility in ten metros at once. Community galas, garba nights and Diwali events are venues where you can measure people in person and be trusted while doing it.

Practically: recruit ambassadors in your top metros, give each a fit kit and a commission, and have them host measurement events. That converts a personal network into distribution, which is the thing most competitors in this space cannot buy.

SequenceWhat to build, and what to deliberately fake
Build plan

The first hundred orders need no software at all

The temptation is to build the marketplace. Don’t — not yet. Every unknown that can kill this business is operational, and software written before those answers is written against guesses.

  1. Concierge, months 0–6, roughly 50–100 orders

    WhatsApp, a Shopify page, a shared spreadsheet. Three vendors you have personally visited. You handle every order by hand. The goal is not revenue — it is discovering the fit failure modes, the true landed cost, and the real transit time. Ship at least one consolidated multi-unit entry alongside single parcels and compare what customs actually does.

  2. Wedding parties, months 4–12

    Ambassador-hosted fit events in three metros. Package pricing for bridal groups. This is where you find out whether the unit economics survive contact with a real customer and whether the guarantee is affordable.

  3. Productise what hurt, months 9–18

    Now build, and only what the manual phase proved you need: the fit profile with its feedback loop, a landed-cost calculator that shows duty before payment, a date-guarantee engine that works backwards from the event, and a vendor portal with spec sheets and a QC photo checklist. Keep ordering on WhatsApp — this customer lives there, and moving her to an app is a tax, not a feature.

  4. Second market, months 18+

    Stand up the second importer entity. If you built step one correctly this is a configuration exercise, not a rewrite.

Validation plan

Cheap experiments that answer the expensive questions

  1. Ship five real orders, end to end

    The single highest-value thing you can do. Real customers, real money, real customs. You learn actual landed cost, actual duty paid, actual transit time and actual fit success in one experiment. Everything else is opinion until this is done.

  2. Ship one parcel and one consolidated ten-unit shipment

    Compare what customs charges each way, and what the brokerage actually costs. This tells you whether the consolidation thesis — the core of the economics table above — is real.

  3. Interview eight boutiques in Delhi and Jaipur

    Do they hold an IEC? What is their made-to-measure capacity in a month? What price would they quote for a foreign customer’s measurements? You are testing whether supply is as willing as assumed.

  4. Interview fifteen customers who had an outfit made in the last year

    Where, what it cost, how long, what went wrong. You are looking for the exact sentence they use to describe the pain — that sentence becomes the headline.

  5. Landing page and a small ad spend

    A few hundred dollars to Indian-American women 25–45 in NJ, NY, Chicago, the Bay Area, Dallas and Atlanta. Measure signup rate, and put a price question in the form — willingness to pay at $349 versus $449 is the number that decides your market.

Bottom line

The demand is real, your access to it is unusually good, and the supply-side gap you identified — that vendors want to sell abroad and structurally cannot — is a genuine and defensible place to stand. The business is viable.

But it is a cross-border operations company wearing a fashion brand’s clothes, not a storefront. The winning version prices duty in, guarantees the fit and the date, and treats the measurement profile as the asset. The losing version competes on Indian prices, discovers the tariff line at month four, and cannot pay for the remakes.

Before spending money on product: run test one. Five real orders will teach you more than any amount of planning, including this brief.
